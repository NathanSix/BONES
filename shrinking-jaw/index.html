Evolution · What the skeleton remembers

The shrinking jaw

Your jaw is one of the smallest in the long line of human relatives. Over two million years our ancestors found ways to chew less: cutting, pounding, cooking, and finally farming soft food. Each step left the face a little smaller. The teeth didn't always shrink to match, which is part of why so many people today have crowded teeth and wisdom teeth with nowhere to go.

The big picture

Two million years of chewing less

Step by step

How the face got smaller

About 2.3 to 1.2 million years ago

The biggest jaws: Paranthropus

A side branch of the human family, nicknamed "Nutcracker Man," had huge molars, a thick jaw and a bony ridge on top of the skull to anchor giant chewing muscles. The nickname turned out to be wrong. Chemical traces in their teeth point to a diet of tough grasses and sedges, ground down slowly, not cracked nuts . They were a different branch from ours, and they died out.

Early stone tools, before regular cooking

Stone tools did the chewing

Slicing meat and pounding root vegetables with stones cut the chewing effort needed by almost 20%, in lab tests with modern volunteers . Tools were a kind of outside teeth.

About 2 million years ago

Smaller teeth, less time eating: Homo erectus

In our own branch, molars got smaller than you'd expect for a body that size, starting around the time of Homo erectus . Modern humans spend about a tenth of the time eating that a primate our size should. Cooking is the leading explanation .

About 300,000 years ago

A face tucked under the brain, and a chin

Our species has a flat face tucked under a rounded braincase, and a bony chin. No other living primate has one . Why we have it is still an open puzzle .

About 12,000 years ago

Farming changed the lower jaw

Across 11 populations worldwide, farmers' lower jaws are shorter and broader than hunter-gatherers', whatever their ancestry . In the earliest farming villages of the Near East, the jaw and the teeth stopped fitting each other well, and crowding shows up .

The last 300 years

Soft bread, a new way of chewing

Teeth from British cemeteries show that people in the industrial era wore their molars differently from medieval people, a sign their jaws moved less from side to side. Softer, more processed bread is the likely reason .

Today

Teeth with nowhere to go

About 1 in 5 people never grow at least one wisdom tooth . Crowded and crooked teeth are common. How much of that is evolution, and how much is how a jaw grows during childhood, is still argued .

The open question

Evolution or growing up?

Two different things could shrink a jaw, and they work on very different clocks.

Slow

Evolution

Over thousands of generations, people with smaller jaws and teeth did fine on softer food, so big jaws stopped being favored. This fits the two-million-year trend, from Homo erectus to us.

Fast

Growth

Bone grows to the work it does. A child who chews soft food grows a jaw with less pull from the chewing muscles. Animals raised on soft food grow smaller faces than the same animals raised on hard food . Changes within a few centuries are too fast to be evolution, so this has to be part of it.

The jaw follows the same rule as every other bone in your body. Load builds it. Take the load away and it builds less.

That's the same idea behind the Move section of BONES. Your legs and spine respond to heavy, weight-bearing work. Your jaw responded to two million years of chewing, and to the last few thousand years of not chewing much at all.

What this page won't tell you. There isn't good evidence that chewing gum, jaw exercises or tongue-posture trends like "mewing" reshape an adult jaw. If you're worried about a child's teeth or bite, that's a question for a dentist or orthodontist, not a website.
