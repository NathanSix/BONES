Chapter 01 · How little you need

Less than you think, done right

The research keeps finding the same shape. Going from nothing to a little brings the biggest drop in risk. After that, each extra hour buys less. You don’t need a gym habit that eats your life. You need the first few hours, every week.

Reading the curve honestly

Why it flattens, and why it bends back up

The steep part at the start is the part that matters for most people. Someone who does nothing and starts doing a little moves the furthest down the curve.

The upturn on the strength curve, past about two hours a week, is harder to read. Few people in these studies lifted that much, so the line there rests on thin data. It could be real, or it could be that people who lift a great deal differ in ways the studies couldn’t measure. Nobody knows yet, and BONES grades it that way.

What this doesn’t mean

It doesn’t mean lifting more than an hour a week is bad for you. It means the evidence for extra benefit past that point is weak, so you don’t need more to get most of the payoff. If you love training, train.

The official numbers

What the guidelines ask for

The World Health Organization’s 2020 guidelines ask adults for 150 to 300 minutes a week of moderate activity, like brisk walking, or 75 to 150 minutes of vigorous activity. On top of that, muscle-strengthening work on two or more days. People over 65 add balance and strength work on three or more days to help prevent falls.

That sounds like a lot until you see how it fits into a week. Two short lifting sessions and a walk most days covers it.

Chapter 02 · Load

Why bones want weight

Your skeleton isn’t scaffolding. It’s living tissue that is torn down and rebuilt all the time, and it rebuilds to match what you ask of it. Weight is the message. No weight, no message.

The thermostat in your bones

Bone keeps score of the load

Bone works like a thermostat for strain. Cells buried in the bone sense how much it bends under load. When the strain stays high, cells called osteoblasts lay down new bone. When it stays low, cells called osteoclasts dissolve bone that isn’t being used. The skeleton you have is a record of the loads you’ve given it.

Each round of digging out old bone and filling in new takes months, which is why bone changes slowly in both directions.

Use it or lose it

Three natural experiments

Take load away. In orbit, nothing pushes back on an astronaut’s legs and spine. On a four-to-six-month mission they lose about 1 to 1.5% of their bone density every month.

Add load to one side. A baseball pitcher throws with one arm, so the other arm is a built-in control. Throwing nearly doubled the strength of the upper-arm bone in the throwing arm. Fifty years after players stopped, about half the extra size and a third of the extra strength were still there.

A pitcher’s two arms

Pitchers still playing.

Cross-sections of the upper-arm bone, drawn to show the direction of the difference, not exact measurements.

Add load at any age. In the LIFTMOR trial, 101 women, around 65 on average, with low bone mass were randomly assigned to one of two programs for 8 months. One group did 30-minute, coached sessions twice a week: heavy deadlifts, squats and overhead presses, 5 sets of 5, plus jumping chin-up drops. The other did a gentle home program. The heavy group’s spine density went up about 2.9%; the gentle group’s went down about 1.2%. The only injury in the heavy group was a minor back spasm.

Coached is the key word. Every LIFTMOR session was supervised, and the loads were built up gradually. If you have osteoporosis, learn the lifts with a qualified coach or physical therapist before you load them.

Patience

Your tissues adapt on different clocks

The first gains come from your nervous system. In one study, leg strength rose almost 40% in five weeks while the muscle itself grew about 7%. Your brain was learning to use the muscle you already had.

Muscle growth shows up on a scan within about three weeks of hard training. Tendons stiffen over weeks to months and thicken over months to years. Bone needs months before a scan can see the change.

The long game

Build it early, keep it late

Bone mass usually peaks somewhere between 18 and 25. After that the job is keeping it. In the five to seven years after menopause, women can lose up to 20% of their bone density, which is why the LIFTMOR result matters so much.

Muscle follows a similar arc. After 30, men can lose as much as 3 to 5% of their muscle mass per decade without training. Load slows both losses.

Chapter 03 · The four lifts

Squat, hinge, push, pull

Four movement patterns load almost every major muscle in the body. Each one has a version you can do today with nothing, a version with a dumbbell or kettlebell, and a version with a barbell. Pick the rung that fits you now. The pattern stays the same.

How much, how heavy

Hard sets, not long sessions

What builds strength is effort near your limit, not time in the gym. Even in people who already lift, a single hard set of 6 to 12 reps, two or three times a week, made them measurably stronger over two to three months. More sets did more, but the first set does a lot of the work.

How heavy? It matters less than people think for muscle. Light and heavy weights grew muscle about equally when people took each set close to their limit. Heavy weights built more maximum strength, because strength is partly a skill of lifting heavy things.

Pick a weight where the last two or three reps are hard, but your form holds. When that gets easy, add a little.

Strength shows up in surprising places. In a study of nearly 140,000 people in 17 countries, every 5 kg lower grip strength was linked to 16% higher risk of death. Grip predicted death better than blood pressure did. It’s a sign of overall strength, not a reason to train your grip on its own.

Lifting also protects you. Across 25 trials, strength training cut sports injuries to about a third.

Chapter 04 · Walking

Walking and the step count

Walking is the movement our bodies are built around. The famous 10,000-step goal turns out to be an advertising slogan. The real research points lower, and it says the first few thousand steps matter most.

Where the number came from

10,000 was a brand name

After the 1964 Tokyo Olympics, a Japanese company sold a pedometer called the manpo-kei: the “10,000-step meter.” The round number stuck, and it traveled the world long before anyone tested it.

When researchers finally did test it, they found benefits that start well below 10,000. People who walked about 8,000 steps a day had about half the risk of death of people who walked 4,000. A 2025 review of 57 studies found most of the benefit by about 7,000 steps, with only modest gains past that for most health outcomes.

Build your day

How many steps is a walk?

A brisk walk runs about 100 steps a minute. That pace is also the usual line for moderate activity, the kind the guidelines count. Set your day below and see where you land.

Count or pace?

Does walking faster add anything?

Maybe. One study found that once you account for total steps, how fast people walked didn’t change their risk. Another found that people whose brisker half hour was faster did better, but other measures of pace showed nothing. Until that’s settled, total steps is the number to watch.

What our bodies expect

The Hadza walk, and burn the same

The Hadza of Tanzania still hunt and gather. GPS trackers showed men walking about 11 km a day and women about 6. The surprise: after accounting for body size, they burned about the same total energy per day as Westerners. The body seems to budget its energy rather than simply burning more as you move more, which may be one reason walking does more for health than for weight on its own.

Walking isn’t the only way in. Cycling, swimming, wheeling and other steady activity count toward the same weekly minutes. Steps are just the easiest thing to measure.

Chapter 05 · Mobility

Range you can control

Flexibility is how far a joint can go. Mobility is how far you can take it yourself, with strength at the end of the range. The second is the one that keeps you off the floor at 80.

What the trials say

Lifting is a stretch

Lifting through a full range of motion improved flexibility about as much as stretching did, across 11 head-to-head studies. You don’t have to choose. A deep squat is a stretch with a weight attached.

Stretching before sport is a ritual, but in trials it didn’t prevent injuries. Strength training did, cutting them to about a third.

For older adults, the biggest risk is a fall. Exercise programs built around balance and everyday movements cut the number of falls by about 23%, and the evidence for that is about as strong as exercise research gets.

Two minutes, no equipment

The sit-to-rise test

Stand barefoot on a soft floor. Sit down cross-legged without leaning on anything, then stand back up the same way. You start with 5 points for sitting and 5 for rising, and lose a point for every hand, knee, forearm or side of the leg you use for support. In a study of 2,002 adults aged 51 to 80, each point on this test was linked to 21% lower risk of death over about six years.

Skip it if you have hip or knee pain, poor balance, or a reason to avoid getting down on the floor. The score is a snapshot of strength, balance and flexibility together, not a diagnosis.

Score yourself

10

Tap a support each time you use it. Half a point comes off for a wobble. A score is for tracking yourself over time, not for comparing with anyone else.

Five minutes, most days

A mobility routine

This isn’t a graded protocol. It’s a short set of positions that take your major joints through their range, under your own control. Do it as a warm-up before lifting, or on its own on walk days.

  • 1 · Hips and anklesDeep squat holdHold the bottom of a squat for 30 to 60 seconds. Hold a door frame if you need to. Shift gently side to side.
  • 2 · Back of the legsSlow hip hingeTen slow hinges, pushing the hips back until you feel the hamstrings, back flat.
  • 3 · Upper backOpen bookLie on your side, knees bent. Rotate your top arm open and back, following it with your eyes. Five each side.
  • 4 · ShouldersWall slideBack against a wall, slide your arms up and down keeping them on the wall. Ten slow reps.
  • 5 · BalanceSingle-leg standStand on one leg for 30 seconds each side, near something you can grab. Close your eyes when it gets easy.
