Orbits that don't close

Under Newton's gravity a planet retraces the same ellipse forever. Relativity adds one term to the force and the ellipse starts to turn. Close in, that term wins: no circular orbit is stable inside 6 M, and a particle that clears the potential barrier simply falls in.

d²r/dτ² = −M/r² + L²/r³ − 3ML²/r⁴

Light that falls

Photons travel through the same curved spacetime. Aim one closer than 3√3 M ≈ 5.196 M and it never comes back. Just outside that line it can loop the hole several times before escaping, which is where the thin bright ring in the image comes from.

Clocks near the hole

Gravity slows time. A clock hovering at radius r ticks at √(1 − 2M/r) of a distant clock. A clock in a circular orbit runs slower still, √(1 − 3M/r), because its orbital speed adds special-relativistic time dilation.

dτ/dt = √(1 − 2M/r)  ·  √(1 − 3M/r) (orbiting)

    Spaghettification

    What kills you near a black hole is the difference in pull between your head and your feet. It grows as M/r³, and at the horizon it scales as 1/M². A small hole shreds you far outside the horizon; a supermassive one lets you cross in one piece.

    What the model includes

    Spacetime and light

    The Schwarzschild metric of a non-rotating, uncharged hole. For every pixel the shader integrates the exact photon orbit equation u″ + u = 3u² with fourth-order Runge–Kutta, backward from the camera, and records where the ray ends: on the disk, in the hole, or on the sky.

    The disk

    Geometrically thin and optically thick, from the innermost stable orbit at 6 M outward. Its radial brightness is the Page–Thorne (1974) profile with zero torque at the inner edge, and each patch glows as a blackbody. Observed temperature is g times local temperature, where g combines gravitational redshift, time dilation and the Doppler shift of gas orbiting at up to half the speed of light.

    Simplifications

    Peak temperature is scaled into visible light; real disks peak in the ultraviolet (supermassive holes) or X-rays (stellar ones). Not modeled: spin (the Kerr metric), light-travel delays across the disk, disk thickness and polarization. The turbulence pattern is procedural, though it orbits at the correct Keplerian rate for each radius.

    Observers

    By default the camera hovers at a fixed radius, which takes a rocket, so incoming starlight is blueshifted. Dive mode switches to an observer falling freely from rest far away; that observer's frame stays valid through the horizon and down to the singularity.
