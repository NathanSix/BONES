Field guide

What you're looking at

The model above is built from OSIRIS-REx's measurements: Bennu's real dimensions, its spinning-top profile, its near-black surface, and a boulder field that follows the size pattern seen from orbit.

ShapeA spinning top

Two rough cones joined at a ridge around the equator. Bennu likely spun faster in the past, and loose rubble slid toward the equator and piled up there.

ColorDarker than charcoal

It reflects only about 4% of the sunlight that hits it, one of the darkest objects in the solar system. The darkness comes from carbon, and carbon-rich rock is exactly where organic molecules survive. That's why NASA picked it.

SurfaceBoulders everywhere

Telescopes suggested smooth, sandy ground. Instead the team found boulders across the whole surface. Some bright ones have veins of carbonate, an early clue to water. The tallest, Benben Saxum, stands about 22 m (72 ft), roughly a seven-story building.

StructureA ball pit, not solid ground

During the sample grab, the arm met almost no resistance, like pushing a French press plunger. It left an 8 m crater, and the spacecraft would have kept sinking if it hadn't fired its thrusters to back away. Bennu is loose rubble held together by very weak gravity.

Sample siteNightingale

A crater about 20 m wide, high in the northern hemisphere. It was picked because it held fine-grained material small enough for the collector to swallow, in a field of boulders that mostly wasn't.

Why NASA watches it1 in 2,700

Bennu has roughly a 1 in 2,700 chance of hitting Earth on September 24, 2182. Learning how a rubble pile behaves helps plan how you'd deflect one.

Mission log

Seven years out and back

  1. Sep 2016

    Launch

    OSIRIS-REx lifts off from Cape Canaveral.

  2. Dec 2018

    Arrival

    Spacecraft reaches Bennu and maps it for nearly two years.

  3. Oct 2020

    Touch and go

    About six seconds of contact. A nitrogen puff kicks surface material into the collector.

  4. Sep 2023

    Capsule lands

    Sample capsule parachutes into the Utah desert, sealed from Earth's air and life.

  5. Jan 2025

    First big papers

    Nucleobases, amino acids, ammonia, and brine salts reported in Nature and Nature Astronomy.

  6. Dec 2025

    Sugars and stardust

    Ribose, glucose, a "space gum" polymer, and an unusual load of supernova dust.

The capsule was opened in a clean room at NASA's Johnson Space Center, inside glove boxes filled with nitrogen so Earth's air and moisture never touched the material. About a quarter of the sample went to the mission's science team, and small shares went to Canada's and Japan's space agencies. Most of the rest is stored for future researchers with better instruments, the same approach NASA took with the Apollo moon rocks.

The spacecraft didn't retire. It dropped the capsule and kept flying, and is now called OSIRIS-APEX, on its way to the asteroid Apophis, which passes unusually close to Earth in April 2029.

Why it had to fly itself

On the day of the grab, Bennu was about 334 million km from Earth. A radio signal took roughly 18.5 minutes each way, far too slow to steer by hand. The spacecraft compared what its cameras saw with a stored map of the site and made its own decisions, including a built-in option to abort if it was heading toward a boulder.

How the letters work

Nucleobases are the rungs of the DNA ladder. Each one pairs with a specific partner, and the order of letters along a strand spells out instructions, the same way letters spell words. RNA uses U where DNA uses T.

Inventory 02 / Protein parts

14 of the 20 amino acids life builds proteins from

Plus 19 more amino acids that Earth life doesn't use, for 33 in total.

Amino acids are beads. A protein is a chain of hundreds of them folded into a shape, and proteins do nearly all the work in a cell. The 19 extras matter: living things make only their 20, while plain chemistry makes a messy variety. A big mix like Bennu's is a sign these formed without life involved.

Why this sample matters

Meteorites that fall to Earth get contaminated by our air, soil, and microbes the moment they land, so you can never be fully sure what came from space. Bennu's sample was sealed in space and opened in a clean lab. What's in it came from the asteroid.

Every part of RNA, in one rock

RNA needs three things: nucleobases (including U), the sugar ribose, and phosphate. Bennu has all of them. That supports the "RNA world" idea: before DNA and proteins, early life may have run on RNA alone, because RNA can both store information and do chemical work. One telling detail: deoxyribose, the sugar DNA needs, was not found. That fits the idea that RNA came first.

Analysis 01 / Chirality

Left hand, right hand

Amino acids come in two mirror-image versions, like a pair of gloves. Same atoms, same bonds, flipped. Below is alanine, one of the amino acids found on Bennu, and its mirror twin.

Carbon (center) Amino group Acid group Methyl Hydrogen
Left: the version life uses. Right: its mirror image. Drag to spin them.

Why it happens: the center carbon holds four different groups. Any time a carbon has four different neighbors, there are two ways to arrange them that are mirror images, and no amount of turning converts one into the other. Chemists call them L (from the Latin for left) and D (for right).

Space makes both. Life picked one.

Every living thing builds proteins from left-handed amino acids only. That shared choice is strong evidence that all life descends from one common ancestor. Why left won is still unsolved. Bennu narrows it down: the delivery was an even mix, so the choice happened here. Sugars go the other way: life's sugars are all the right-handed (D) form.

Leading ideas for why left won

  • Polarized starlightSome light in star-forming regions is twisted in one direction, and it can destroy one hand slightly faster than the other. A small tilt to start with.
  • Chance, then amplificationA tiny random excess got locked in, and certain reactions make the more common hand even more common.
  • Mineral surfacesSome crystal faces grip one hand better, sorting molecules as they formed.

None of these is proven. They may have worked together.

Analysis 02 / Water

A wet past, written in salt

Bennu is a fragment of a much bigger parent body. The salts and clays in the sample show that parent body once held pockets of liquid brine. Slide through its history.

How we know there was water

  • Evaporite saltsIncluding sodium carbonate, the same white crust that forms on dry lake beds like Searles Lake in California's Mojave Desert. It had never been seen in a meteorite or any space sample before.
  • Clays and carbonatesMinerals that only form when water soaks into rock and changes it.
  • Bright veins on bouldersSeen from orbit before the sample came home: carbonate filling old cracks where fluid once flowed.

These salts dissolve in humid air. A meteorite falling through Earth's atmosphere would lose them, which is why they only showed up in a sealed sample.

Analysis 03 / Origins

Older than the Sun

The December 2025 papers found grains of dust that formed before our solar system existed, some blasted out of exploding stars. Scientists spot these "presolar grains" by their isotopes: the ratios of heavier and lighter versions of the same elements don't match anything made in our solar system, so they carry the chemical signature of the star that made them.

Bennu's parent body had about six times more supernova dust than any space material studied before. That suggests it formed in a patch of the early solar system unusually rich in debris from dead stars.

Put it together and the ingredients for life were cooked from star debris and spread through the early solar system. They weren't rare, and they weren't made only on Earth.

Frontier / Mirror life

What if someone built a right-handed cell?

Your immune system recognizes invaders by shape, the way a key fits a lock. A bacterium built from mirror-image parts would present flipped shapes. Run the test.

Shape fits. Alarm raised. The immune system attacks.

The same mismatch would apply to animals, plants, and fungi, because all of them run on the same left-handed chemistry. Digestive enzymes are gloves too, so microbes and viruses that normally keep bacteria in check might not be able to break one down. With no predators and no immune response, a mirror microbe could spread unchecked. That's why the worry is about whole ecosystems, not just people.

Mirror molecules on their own aren't the problem. Drug researchers already use mirror-image proteins because the body's enzymes can't break them down, so they last longer. The line scientists drew is at a mirror cell that can copy itself.

What Bennu does and doesn't tell us

Shows
  • Life's raw ingredients form naturally in space.
  • They were common in the early solar system.
  • Water, organics, and minerals once mixed inside asteroids.
  • Space delivers both molecular hands in roughly equal amounts.
Doesn't show
  • That life exists anywhere else.
  • That life on Earth started from asteroid deliveries.
  • That life ever began on Bennu or its parent body.
  • Why life chose left-handed amino acids.
