Chapter 01 · Origins

How we came to eat this way

Humans are the animal that processes its food. Stone tools, then fire, then farms, ships and factories each changed what we eat, and our bodies changed with them.

Deep time

Three million years on one line

The scale below is logarithmic: each step to the left is ten times further back, so stone tools and last decade’s drug trials fit on the same line. Tap a point, or use the arrows.

Anatomy

A body built for easy food

Compared with other primates, we spend almost no time eating, our molars are small for our size, and our gut is built around the small intestine, where easily digested food is absorbed. Something made food softer and richer long before farming.

Cooking is the leading suspect. The trouble is the dates: teeth shrank about 1.9 million years ago, but the oldest secure fire is about 1 million years old and the oldest cooked meal about 780,000.

Still argued: the idea that our bigger brains were paid for with smaller guts. A study of about 100 mammals found the trade-off ran through fat stores instead.

Foragers

There was never one paleo diet

Hunter-gatherers ate whatever their place offered. In the Kalahari that meant mostly plants; in the Arctic, almost entirely meat and fish. The same ethnographic records that paleo-diet books cite show the range.

For all that walking, Hadza adults burn about as many calories a day as adults in the US and Europe, once body size is accounted for.

A larger study found daily calorie burn rises with activity at first and then levels off: the most active people burned about as much as the moderately active. Exercise is still one of the best things you can do for your health; it just isn’t the main lever on how much you burn.

Domestication

Farming narrowed the menu

Every staple crop started as a wild plant that people bred, year after year, for bigger and easier harvests. Maize is the clearest case. Its wild ancestor, teosinte, still grows in Mexico, and its ear looks nothing like corn.

Farming fed far more people, and it concentrated the world’s plate on a handful of plants. Of more than 6,000 species people grow for food, nine supply two thirds of all crop production.

The rest of the farming story, from the first fields to what grain did to teeth and height, is told in Staying Put, the BONES explainer on the move to farming.

The Columbian exchange

1492: the great swap

Until 1492, the Americas and the rest of the world farmed separately, with different crops and animals. Within a century of Columbus the two were mixed together, and much of what we think of as national cuisine is the result.

Hungarian paprika, Italian tomato sauce and West African cacao all come from plants first grown in the Americas.

Industrial food

The factory kitchen

In about a century, a handful of inventions made food cheap, shelf-stable and abundant: the sealed can, the roller mill that makes white flour, hydrogenated fat, and fertilizer pulled from the air. Tap an item as it passes.

Now

Ultra-processed

Ultra-processed foods are industrial formulations: mostly refined ingredients plus additives you wouldn’t find in a kitchen, like flavorings, emulsifiers and sweeteners. Sodas, packaged snacks, most breakfast cereals and many breads count. They are now most of what Americans eat.

Does processing itself matter, or just the sugar, fat and salt that come with it? In 2019 an NIH team tested that directly. Twenty adults lived on a research ward for four weeks, two weeks on each diet, The meals put in front of them were matched for calories, sugar, fat, fiber, salt and carbohydrate, but nobody had to finish them: they could eat as much or as little as they liked. On the ultra-processed diet they chose to eat about 500 more calories a day.

Where to draw the line is still argued. Most research uses NOVA, a four-group system that sorts foods by how and why they were processed. Slide through it below.

Not all packaged food is ultra-processed. Canned beans, frozen vegetables, plain oats and plain yogurt are minimally processed, and they show up again in the shopping chapter as some of the best food in the store.

Chapter 02 · Labels

Reading the package

A package has two sides. The back is regulated: the Nutrition Facts label and the ingredient list have to follow federal rules. The front is mostly marketing. Start with the back.

Nutrition Facts

Read it from the top

This is FDA’s own sample label, for a frozen lasagna. Work down it in order, or tap any line. Then change how many servings you actually eat: everything on the label, including the percentages, scales with it.

Ingredients

The list is in order of weight

Ingredients have to be listed from most to least by weight, so the first two or three tell you what the food mostly is. For anything grain-based, one quick test does better than the stamps on the front: divide total carbohydrate by fiber. Ten or less is a good sign.

The front of the box

What the words legally mean

Some front-of-package words have strict legal definitions. Others have none at all. Tap each sticker on this made-up cereal box to see which is which.

Proposed

The box that may come next

In January 2025 FDA proposed a small box for the front of most packages, rating saturated fat, sodium and added sugars as Low, Medium or High. It uses the same 5% and 20% cutoffs as the back label. It had not been finalized as of mid-2026.

The salt shaker is a small part of the sodium story. About 71% of the sodium Americans eat is already in packaged and restaurant food; about 11% is added at home.

Date stamps

“Best by” is not “bad after”

Apart from infant formula, date labels aren’t required by federal law, and most are about quality, not safety. Tap a stamp to flip it.

Definitions from USDA’s Food Safety and Inspection Service. USDA estimates about 30% of the food supply is lost or wasted at stores and in homes, and date confusion is one reason.

Official advice

What the guidelines say now

The Dietary Guidelines for Americans are rewritten every five years. The 2025–2030 edition, released in January 2026, made some notable changes.

Protein1.2–1.6 g/kgof body weight a day. The RDA, which is a minimum, is 0.8 g/kg.
Saturated fat≤ 10%of calories, unchanged
Added sugar≤ 10 gper meal
Sodium< 2,300 mga day
Highly processed foodLimit“significantly,” in the guidelines’ own word
Cooking fatOlive oillisted first, with butter or beef tallow as other options

Where experts disagree

Cardiology and nutrition researchers welcomed the push on added sugar and processed food. They objected to naming butter and beef tallow as cooking fats and to leading with red meat for protein, and Harvard’s nutrition department noted that typical servings in the plan can push saturated fat past the guidelines’ own 10% limit. The evidence on fat types is laid out in the macros chapter, so you can judge for yourself.

Chapter 03 · Shopping

How to shop

Much of the best value in a grocery store is plain food: beans, oats, frozen vegetables, eggs, canned fish. Here is a walk through the aisles, how to compare prices, and which foods give you the most per calorie.

The store

A walk through the aisles

Most stores follow the same plan: fresh food around the walls, packaged food in the middle. That isn’t a rule to shop only the edges; some of the best buys are in the middle aisles. Tap a section.

Price

Read the small number on the shelf tag

The big number is the package price. The small one, the unit price, is the price per ounce or pound, and it is the only fair way to compare sizes. It is voluntary in most of the US; 16 states have rules for it. Guess which of each pair is cheaper per ounce, then tap it.

Prices are made up for the exercise; the arithmetic is the point. Bigger is often cheaper per unit, but not always.

Per calorie

Protein and fiber in every 100 calories

Most foods are good at one or the other. Meat, fish and dairy pack protein but no fiber; fruit, grains and vegetables bring fiber with less protein. Beans, lentils and peas sit near the top right, which is why they keep coming up.

    Chapter 04 · Macros

    Each macro, explained

    Protein, fat and carbohydrate supply nearly all your energy. Fiber, alcohol and water round out the picture. For each one: what it does, how much the evidence supports, and where the arguments are.

    CaloriesProteinFatCarbohydrateFiberSugarAlcoholWater

    Energy

    Calories, briefly

    A calorie is a unit of energy. Each gram of protein or carbohydrate supplies about 4, fat about 9 and alcohol about 7. Fiber isn’t digested by you, but gut bacteria ferment some of it, which yields about 2.

    That’s why foods differ so much in how much you get to eat. A hundred calories of broccoli fills a bowl; a hundred calories of olive oil is under a tablespoon.

    Protein

    Protein: building blocks you can’t store

    Proteins are chains of amino acids. Your body breaks the protein you eat back into amino acids, then builds its own: muscle, enzymes, antibodies, hormones. There are 20 amino acids, and nine are essential, meaning they have to come from food.

    How much? The RDA is 0.8 g per kg of body weight a day, a floor that covers nearly everyone. The 2025–2030 guidelines raised the target to 1.2–1.6 g/kg. For people who lift weights, pooled trials found extra protein helped muscle growth up to about 1.6 g/kg, then stopped adding much.

    100 gof protein after a workout built more muscle protein, for longer, than 25 g. The “30 g at a time” limit didn’t hold.
    No harmto kidney function from high-protein diets in people with healthy kidneys. Kidney disease is a different case.

    Fat

    Fat: it’s all about shape

    Most fat in food is made of fatty acids: chains of carbon atoms with hydrogen attached. A saturated chain has no double bonds, so it lies straight, and straight chains stack tightly into a solid. A cis double bond puts a kink in the chain; kinked chains can’t pack, so the fat stays liquid.

    Shape is also why trans fats were invented and then banned. Partial hydrogenation, patented in 1902, flipped kinked oils into straight trans chains, making cheap solid fats like shortening. Trans fats raised heart disease risk so clearly that FDA ruled the oils unsafe in 2015.

    For saturated fat, the case runs through LDL cholesterol. LDL causes the plaque that leads to heart attacks; that is one of the best-supported findings in medicine. Saturated fat raises LDL, and in long trials, cutting it lowered cardiovascular events by 17%. What replaces it matters most: swapping in polyunsaturated fat lowered heart disease by about 19%.

    Still argued

    Seed oils like soybean and canola are high in polyunsaturated fat. Trials and blood studies favor them; a few older trials recovered decades later point the other way, which keeps the argument going. The weight of evidence is in the myth deck. Seed oil card

    Carbohydrate

    Carbohydrate: sugar, starch and fiber

    Sugars are single or paired sugar units. Starch is long chains of glucose, which your gut snips back into glucose. Fiber is carbohydrate your enzymes can’t break, so it travels on to the colon. The brain alone runs on about 130 g of glucose a day, which sets the carbohydrate RDA; below that, the body makes glucose from other sources.

    Glycemic index measures how much a food’s carbohydrate raises blood sugar compared with pure glucose. Glycemic load adjusts that for a normal serving. Some results surprise people: whole wheat bread scores about the same as white.

    Fiber

    Fiber: the nutrient almost everyone is short on

    The recommendation is 14 g per 1,000 calories, about 28 g on a 2,000-calorie day. More than 90% of women and 97% of men in the US fall short. People eating 25–29 g a day had 15–30% lower risk of death and heart disease than those eating the least.

    Sugar

    Sugar: where it’s free, and where it isn’t

    WHO advises keeping free sugars under 10% of calories, and under 5%, about 25 g, for extra benefit. Free sugars are added sugars plus honey, syrups and fruit juice; the sugar inside whole fruit and milk doesn’t count. The US label’s Daily Value for added sugars is 50 g, and the new guidelines say no more than 10 g per meal.

    A myth worth dropping: sugar doesn’t make kids hyperactive. Sixteen double-blind studies found no effect.

    Alcohol

    Alcohol: the fourth macro

    Alcohol is energy too: about 7 calories a gram. A US standard drink holds about 14 g of it, so roughly 100 calories before any sugar in the mixer.

    Alcohol is linked to at least seven cancers, and the US Surgeon General asked for cancer warnings on labels in January 2025. The old idea that a drink a day helps you live longer came largely from studies that counted former drinkers, often people who quit because they were sick, as nondrinkers.

    Water

    Water: food counts too

    The adequate intake for total water is about 3.7 liters a day for men and 2.7 for women, and about a fifth of it comes from food. Nobody ever tested “eight glasses a day.” And coffee counts: in a crossover trial, four mugs a day hydrated as well as water.
