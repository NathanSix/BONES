Other senses

You are not seeing the world as it is. You are seeing the slice your eyes, ears, nose and skin can pick up. Every other animal around you lives inside a different slice, and none of them is the true one. This page walks through a few of those slices, and what our own noise and light do to them.

Every colored chip on this page is a graded claim. Tap one to see the evidence behind it, or read how we grade claims.

The big idea

Every animal lives in a bubble

In the early 1900s the German biologist Jakob von Uexküll gave this idea a name. He called the slice of the world an animal can sense its Umwelt, German for "surroundings" . Everything outside it might as well not exist for that animal.

His favorite example was a tick. Tap through its life the way Uexküll told it.

Your world is a bubble too. It's just a bigger one, so it's easy to forget it has edges.

Sight

Colors you will never see

Light comes in wavelengths, measured in nanometers (nm). Your eyes catch roughly 380 to 700 nm, the band from violet to red . Everything shorter is ultraviolet. Everything longer is infrared. Both are all around you and invisible to you.

Reindeer in the Arctic can see into the ultraviolet too . In snow-bright winter light, lichen they eat and the fur of wolves absorb ultraviolet and stand out dark against the white. A wolf that blends in for us would look like a dark shape on bright snow.

Then there is the mantis shrimp, famous online for having 12 kinds of color receptor to our 3. That sounds like a world of colors we can't imagine. When researchers actually tested them, they turned out to be worse than us at telling similar colors apart . Try the test yourself.

Myth or truth

"Mantis shrimp see millions of colors we can't."

Not supported . More receptors didn't mean finer color vision. The leading idea is that they use their receptors for fast, simple recognition, like a barcode scanner, instead of comparing colors the way our brains do.

Hearing

A world too low and too high

Sound is measured in hertz (Hz), vibrations per second. The chart below puts animals' hearing on one scale . The shaded band is yours. Tap a row to compare.

Elephants rumble below the bottom of your hearing. Bats, mice, porpoises and that small moth live in sounds far above the top of it. A quiet summer night is not quiet. It's just quiet to you.

Senses we don't have

Heat, current, fields and wakes

Some animals sense things we have no organ for at all. A few of the best studied:

Pit vipers feel heat

Rattlesnakes have two pits on their face that work like heat cameras. A thin membrane in each pit warms up from the body heat of nearby animals, and a heat-sensing protein called TRPA1 turns that warmth into a nerve signal. They can strike a mouse in total darkness.

Bees feel electricity

Flying bees pick up a positive charge. Flowers carry a negative one. Bumblebees can detect the tiny electric field around a flower, and they learned a task faster when that field was a clue. A flower's charge also shifts for a few minutes after a bee visits.

Robins may see magnetism

Migrating birds navigate by Earth's magnetic field. A protein in the eye of the European robin, cryptochrome 4, reacts to magnetic fields in the lab. Whether this is how birds actually sense north is still unproven.

Star-nosed moles touch fast

22 fleshy rays around the nose

Covered in about 25,000 touch domes, the star works like a hand and an eye at once. The mole can find and eat a bit of food in as little as 120 milliseconds.

Seals read the water

35 seconds after a fin swam past

Anything swimming leaves a wake. A blindfolded harbor seal used its whiskers to tell which way an artificial fin had gone, even 35 seconds later.

Your nose is better than you think

The idea that humans have a poor sense of smell traces back to a 19th-century brain anatomist, not to experiments. Human smell holds up well against other mammals for many odors. Dogs still win on others.

Sensory pollution

We are loud and bright

Here is where the bubbles collide. We built a world lit and noisy for our own senses, and every other animal has to live inside it too. Most of that damage is invisible to us for the same reason the ultraviolet bullseye is. It happens outside our bubble.

80%+of people live under light-polluted night skies
1 in 3people can no longer see the Milky Way from home
365–988Mbirds killed by building collisions each year in the US, many drawn in by lights at night

Sea turtle hatchlings find the ocean by heading for the brightest, lowest horizon, which used to be moonlight on water. Beachfront lights pull them the wrong way .

The ocean is loud too. After the September 11, 2001 attacks, shipping in Canada's Bay of Fundy dropped and the water got quieter. Stress hormones in the endangered right whales living there fell at the same time .

Good news hiding in this. Light and noise are the easiest kinds of pollution to fix. Turn them off and they are gone the same night. Nothing has to be cleaned up.

What this means for you

Notice your edges

None of this asks you to feel small. It asks you to remember that your senses were shaped for one job, keeping one kind of ape alive, and that the world is much bigger than that job. A few ways to put it into practice:

  1. Go somewhere dark. Find the nearest spot with little light pollution and let your eyes adjust for 20 minutes or more. See how many more stars appear.
  2. Turn off outside lights you don't need, especially during spring and fall bird migration. Many cities run "Lights Out" programs for exactly this.
  3. Take a smell walk. Your nose is better than you've been told. Try naming five smells on one block.
  4. Look closer at one plant. The flower you walk past daily is a billboard aimed at someone else.

How we grade claims

The same five tiers used across BONES. For animal senses, most evidence comes from lab and field experiments on a few species.

TierRule
T1 StrongMany independent studies, species or methods agree.
T2 ModerateOne strong study, or a solid finding in a small number of animals.
T3 ObservationalThe pattern is real. The cause is an educated guess.
T4 ContestedCredible researchers read the evidence differently, or a key step is unproven.
T5 Not supportedThe evidence points the other way.
