Being human · Chapter 4

Staying put

For almost all of our history, humans followed their food. About 12,000 years ago some of us stopped and started growing it. Farming fed more people than the world had ever held. It also reshaped our bodies, and our skeletons kept a record of every change.

300,000 years in one day

A very late start

Squeeze the whole story of our species into a single day. Homo sapiens appears at midnight, about 300,000 years ago . Tap through the day and watch how late everything else shows up.

Farming fills the last hour. Everything you think of as history fits inside it. Most of the body you live in was already built in the 23 hours before. That last hour still left marks of its own, and they are what this chapter is about.

15,000 years ago

They settled first

In the hills of the Levant, around today's Israel, Jordan, Lebanon and Syria, lived people archaeologists call the Natufians. About 15,000 years ago they started doing something new. They built round stone houses, buried their dead close by, and stayed .

They hunted gazelle and harvested wild wheat and barley with flint sickles, then ground the grain on stone. At a site in Jordan called Shubayqa 1, archaeologists found charred crumbs of a bread-like food made 14,400 years ago . That is four thousand years before anyone planted a field.

Farther north, in what is now Turkey, people began raising the giant carved pillars of Göbekli Tepe about 11,600 years ago . Every animal carved on them is wild. The builders mostly hunted and gathered, and archaeologists still argue over whether they lived there year round .

So which came first, the village or the farm? In the Levant, the village. Settling down and farming grew up together over thousands of years, each one pushing the other along.

About 11,500 years ago

The mutation that fed the world

Wild wheat has a clever trick. When its seeds ripen, the ear breaks apart and drops them on the ground, ready to sprout. Botanists call it shattering.

Now and then a plant carries a mutation that keeps its seeds stuck on. In the wild that is a dead end. Then people arrived with sickles. A harvester cuts the whole stalk and carries it home, so the tough ears make it into the basket while the shattering ones mostly fall on the way. Sow the basket next year and the field holds a few more tough ears. Repeat for enough harvests and the whole field changes .

Without meaning to, people built a plant that needs them. Domesticated wheat holds on to its seed, so it can't replant itself. Our crops depend on us as much as we depend on them.

How long did it take?

Researchers disagree . Charred grain from early sites points to a slow drift of 2,000 to 3,000 years. Harvest experiments suggest it could have happened in 20 to 200 years if people picked tough ears on purpose. Slide to see how a small edge at each harvest adds up.

11,500 to 4,000 years ago

Many places, one idea

Farming wasn't invented once and passed around. People worked it out on their own in at least seven regions of the world, each with its own local plants .

Eastern North America is on the list. Long before maize arrived from Mexico, people there had domesticated squash, sunflower and goosefoot .

Read in bone

What farming did to our bodies

We can't interview the first farmers. We can read their skeletons. Bone is living tissue. It thickens where it is loaded, thins where it isn't, and records hunger, sickness and hard work while a person grows. Line up skeletons from before and after farming reached a region, and the same story shows up again and again.

The likely reason is load. Foragers walked and carried far more, and bone builds where it is worked .

Why this one is graded carefully. It fits the BONES message almost too well. The pattern in the bones is solid. The reason behind it is an educated guess, so it gets the same tier it would get if it argued against us.

Forager to early farmer

Farming changed the work

Before you picture early farmers taking it easy, look at their arms. Women farming in Central Europe 7,400 to 7,000 years ago had upper-arm bones stronger for their size than today's elite university rowers .

The leading guess is grinding grain by hand, pushing a stone back and forth for hours a day . Foragers walked more. Early farmers worked their arms harder.

A strange bargain

Here is the puzzle. Early farmers were often shorter and sicker than the foragers before them. Farming spread anyway, because a field feeds far more mouths than wild land and families grew larger. More people, each a little worse off .

The last 10,000 years

We kept evolving

Twelve thousand years is a blink for evolution. It was still long enough for natural selection to leave fingerprints in our DNA, and ancient genomes let us watch some of them form.

Spread with dairying

Milk after childhood

Most mammals lose the ability to digest milk sugar after weaning, and so do most people. A genetic switch called lactase persistence keeps it on. It evolved more than once and spread in milk-using groups across Europe, Africa, the Middle East and Asia. About one adult in three has it today.

The twist: Europeans used milk for thousands of years before the gene became common. Famine and disease seem to explain its spread better than milk drinking alone.

Rose in farming populations

More starch genes

AMY1 makes the enzyme in your spit that starts breaking down starch. Extra copies may go back more than 800,000 years, before we split from Neanderthals. After farming, people with more copies did better. In Europe the average rose from about four copies to seven over 12,000 years.

What your own copy number means for your health today is still unknown.

Arrived with farmers

Lighter skin in Europe

Hunter-gatherers in western and central Europe mostly had darker skin than Europeans today. Farmers moving in from Anatolia from about 8,000 years ago carried light-skin gene variants, and selection kept favoring one of them after the two groups mixed.

One idea for why: hunters got vitamin D from fish and meat, while grain-eating farmers had to make more of it from sunlight.

Borrowed from neighbors

Immune genes

As farmers spread into Europe, they mixed with the hunter-gatherers already living there. Ancient DNA shows selection favored some immune genes the farmers picked up from those neighbors.

Crowded villages next to herds were a new world for germs, and our immune systems had to keep up.

Myth or truth?

Human evolution stopped when we started farming.

Myth. Milk digestion, starch genes, skin color and immune genes all changed within the farming era. Most of your anatomy is far older than farming. Evolution never paused, though.

A big idea

Did wheat domesticate us?

Sapiens · Yuval Noah Harari

Harari argues wheat got the better deal. It spread across the planet, while the people tending it worked longer days, ate a narrower diet and got sicker.

The Dawn of Everything · David Graeber and David Wengrow

Graeber and Wengrow push back. They argue many groups farmed a little, stopped, and started again over thousands of years, so there was no single moment when a trap snapped shut.

This is an argument between thinkers, not a measured finding, so it gets no tier. Read both and decide for yourself. Both live in the Library.

Back to you

What this means for you

MOVE

Forager skeletons were built by daily load, and yours still answers to load the same way. That's why BONES leans on short, heavy, weight-bearing work.

Read: How little you need
FUEL

Grains and milk aren't strangers to human biology. Many people carry genes that adapted to them in the last 10,000 years. The bigger change is how narrow our diet became. Three grasses, rice, wheat and maize, now supply about half of all the calories people eat .

Go to Fuel
THINKING

Be wary of anyone selling a single diet that humans were built for. Foragers ate very different things depending on where they lived, and we kept adapting after farming began.

How we grade history

Health pages on BONES grade claims by trials. History can't be run as a trial, so this chapter uses the same five tiers with rules adapted for bones, DNA and digs.

TierRule for history
T1 StrongMany independent sites, samples or methods agree.

T3 ObservationalThe pattern is real. The reason given for it is an educated guess.
